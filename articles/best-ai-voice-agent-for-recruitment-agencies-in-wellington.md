## What "best" means for a Wellington agency

Voice-agent vendors compete on demos, but the demo is the easy part. For a recruitment agency the value lands in three unglamorous places: whether the call outcome arrives in your ATS as a structured record, whether the agent can screen a large batch of applicants consistently, and whether someone is tuning it as real screening calls come in. An agent that produces transcripts nobody reads is not better than voicemail, however good the voice sounds.

Wellington changes the emphasis in a specific way. The capital's recruitment market leans heavily on government departments, Crown entities, policy and IT contracting, where a single advertised role can draw a large field and where the client cares as much about how candidate data is handled as about the shortlist itself. So the best option for a Wellington desk is the one that screens volume without drifting, and that can survive a procurement questionnaire about where recordings and transcripts live. ## The five criteria to score every option against

**1. ATS write-back.** The single biggest separator. Ask every vendor to demonstrate a write into your exact system (JobAdder, Bullhorn, Vincere or your CRM), creating or updating the specific fields your consultants actually use. "It integrates with an ATS" is not an answer. Ask what happens when the API is down mid-call.

**2. Latency and voice quality.** Cheap stacks introduce pauses that make candidates hang up. A screening call is an employer-brand touchpoint, and in a small market like Wellington word travels, so production-quality voice and low-latency pipelines earn their higher per-minute cost. Test on a real phone line, not a browser demo, because mobile networks expose latency a laptop hides.

**3. High-volume screening throughput.** For Wellington recruitment this is often the strongest single argument for a voice agent. A public-sector vacancy can attract dozens of applicants who all deserve a consistent first-round screen. An agent runs the same structured questions at nine in the morning and nine at night, so a field of forty is screened as evenly as a field of four, and consultants inherit a ranked, structured queue instead of a voicemail backlog.

**4. Compliance and data residency.** The Privacy Act 2020 applies to candidate data an AI agent collects exactly as it applies to a consultant's notes. Recording a call you are party to is lawful in New Zealand, but disclosure and sound data handling belong in the greeting and the flow, not bolted on later. In Wellington the bar is often higher, because government and Crown-entity clients ask where call recordings, transcripts and candidate records are stored and processed. Be ready to answer that question before you win the work, not after. If your agency also runs an Australian desk, several states require all-party consent, so the safe default is to disclose recording at the start of every call.

**5. Ownership after go-live.** The best agent in month one degrades by month three if nobody tunes it. Decide before you buy whether that owner is your team (self-serve) or the partner (managed). This is the real build-versus-buy question, not the software licence.

## Build it yourself or buy it managed

Self-serve platforms such as Retell and Vapi are genuinely strong foundations. They give you the call handling, the voice models and the flow builder, and usage sits around USD 0.07 to USD 0.30 per call minute plus telephony. If you have a technical person who can own prompts, integrations and monitoring, building on one of these can be the best-value route and keeps you closest to the underlying platform.

The catch is that candidate screening is not a simple flow. It branches on availability, right-to-work status, security-clearance eligibility for government roles, notice periods and rate expectations, and the results have to land somewhere consultants will act on them. Most Wellington agencies do not carry spare engineering capacity, which is why a managed agent (a partner builds and owns it on top of a platform like these) is the more common buy. ## FAQ

### How are AI voice agents transforming recruitment in Wellington?

The change is coverage and consistency rather than replacement. Agents answer every inbound candidate call including evenings and weekends, run identical structured screenings so every applicant to a large public-sector field is asked the same questions, and write structured results into the ATS immediately. Consultants spend their time on shortlists and client work instead of phone tag, which matters most when a single government vacancy lands dozens of applicants at once.

### Where is candidate data stored when a government client asks?

That is the question a Wellington agency should settle before it buys, not after. Ask any vendor exactly where call recordings, transcripts and derived candidate records are stored and processed, and whether that can be pinned to a region that satisfies your client's procurement team. A managed partner should be able to give you a written answer and reflect it in the call flow's disclosure, so a Privacy Act obligation and a client's data-residency expectation are handled in the same place.

### Can a non-engineer build the call flows for a recruitment agent?

Up to a point. The no-code builders on the main platforms handle straightforward branching well, but recruitment screening logic and reliable ATS write-back usually reach a wall where someone technical is needed. This is exactly the line that separates a self-serve build from a managed one, so be honest about your team's capacity before committing to run it yourself.

### Will candidates in Wellington actually talk to an AI voice agent?

Completion rates on well-built screening agents are consistently strong when the agent is upfront about what it is, keeps the call under ten minutes and offers a human follow-up. Candidates tolerate AI screening the way they tolerate online application forms. What they do not tolerate is silence, and an agency that screens instantly beats one that calls back in two days.

### Is it legal to record AI screening calls in New Zealand?

Yes, with conditions. Recording a call you are party to is lawful in New Zealand, but candidate data handling must comply with the Privacy Act 2020. Disclose recording at the start of every call and store candidate data the way you would any sensitive record. If you also screen candidates in Australia, treat all-party consent as the default because several states require it.
