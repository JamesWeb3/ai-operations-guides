# AI Governance Framework for Australian Businesses: What to Adopt and in What Order

> An AI governance framework for an Australian business is not one document you buy: it is three layers you assemble in order. The base layer is the law that already applies, chiefly the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles, including the cross-border rule in Australian Privacy Principle 8. The operating layer is the federal Voluntary AI Safety Standard, ten guardrails published in 2024 that tell an Australian organisation what good AI governance looks like, run in practice with the free NIST AI Risk Management Framework. The proof layer, added only when a buyer or tender asks, is certification against ISO/IEC 42001, available here through bodies accredited by JAS-ANZ. Build them in that sequence, because each layer produces the evidence the next one reuses. Across Sentry AI's own Search Console data for the ninety days to 16 September 2026, more than thirty distinct search queries naming ISO/IEC 42001 surfaced our site, several of them explicitly Australian, which is a demand signal that is almost entirely unmet on the first page of results.

For an Australian business, the practical AI governance framework is a stack, not a single named standard. Start by mapping your AI use against the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles, which already bind any system that touches personal information. Then adopt the Voluntary AI Safety Standard as your operating framework, using the NIST AI RMF to do the actual work of identifying and controlling AI risk, because it is free and technology-neutral. Then, and only when a customer, government tender or regulated counterparty wants independent proof, certify against ISO/IEC 42001. The rest of this guide explains why Australia's legal backdrop makes this the right order, what each layer does, and how to tell which layer you are ready for.

## Why Australia has no AI Act, and why that matters

Australia has taken a deliberately staged approach to artificial intelligence. There is no AI-specific statute in force and no single AI Act yet, so the binding obligations sit inside laws you are almost certainly already subject to. The Privacy Act 1988 and its Australian Privacy Principles are the load-bearing ones: they govern any AI system that collects, uses or discloses personal information. Australian Privacy Principle 8 restricts disclosing personal information to an overseas recipient unless you take reasonable steps to ensure comparable protection, and that rule engages the moment you route data through an offshore model provider, which nearly every Australian business does. Recent privacy reform also moves towards requiring transparency about automated decisions that significantly affect people, which your governance needs to be able to surface rather than bury.

The absence of a dedicated AI Act does not mean the absence of duties. It means the duties are distributed across privacy, consumer, anti-discrimination and sector law rather than gathered into one place, and that the federal government has signalled the direction of travel: voluntary guardrails now, with consultation already run on making guardrails mandatory for high-risk AI. A governance framework is how you make those scattered and emerging obligations operational, instead of hoping none of them surfaces in a complaint or a tender questionnaire.

## The three layers of the framework

Think of AI governance in Australia as three layers that build on each other, each answering a different question.

| Layer | What it is | The question it answers | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Legal base | Privacy Act 1988, Australian Privacy Principles, sector rules | What am I already required to do? | None, it applies by default |
| Operating framework | Voluntary AI Safety Standard, run with the NIST AI RMF | How do I organise the ongoing work? | Free to adopt |
| Proof | ISO/IEC 42001 certification | How do I prove it to someone who cannot take my word? | Implementation plus audit fees |

The order matters because each layer produces the evidence the next one reuses. Governing personal data under the Privacy Act creates the data-flow records the operating framework needs. Running the guardrails and the NIST functions produces the operating evidence an ISO 42001 auditor asks to see. Jumping to certification before the lower layers run is how businesses end up paying for an audit they are not ready to pass.

## The operating layer is Australia's own guardrails

What makes the Australian framework distinct from the New Zealand one is that Australia has published its own operating standard. The federal Voluntary AI Safety Standard, released in 2024, sets out ten guardrails covering accountability, risk management, data governance, testing, human oversight, transparency and record-keeping. It is voluntary, but it is the clearest statement of what a regulator expects good practice to look like, and it maps closely onto both ISO/IEC 42001 and the direction of the proposed mandatory guardrails for high-risk settings. Adopting it now is the cheapest way to be ready if those guardrails become mandatory.

The guardrails tell you what good looks like; they do not tell you how to organise the work. That is where the NIST AI RMF comes in, with its four functions (Govern, Map, Measure and Manage) that turn the guardrails into a repeatable method. Most Australian teams use the guardrails as the "what" and the NIST framework as the "how". The detail of those four functions, and why a US framework is the sensible default here, is set out in our guide to [the NIST AI Risk Management Framework for Australian businesses](nist-ai-risk-management-framework-for-australian-businesses.md). The first move under either is an inventory of the AI already in use, including the tools staff adopted without asking, paired with a written policy and named owners.

## Certify with ISO 42001 when a buyer asks

ISO/IEC 42001, published in December 2023, is the first certifiable management-system standard written for AI. It asks you to build and operate an Artificial Intelligence Management System: policies, roles, impact assessments and Annex A controls covering data governance, human oversight of consequential decisions, logging and traceability, and transparency to affected people. Because it is issued by a certification body accredited in Australia through JAS-ANZ, it converts your internal governance into something a buyer or auditor can verify without trusting your description of it.

You do not certify ahead of demand. You certify when a customer, an all-of-government tender or a regulated counterparty wants assurance they can check, at which point the guardrail and framework work you already did becomes most of the audit evidence. What the audit involves, the accreditation route and the indicative fees are set out in our guide to [ISO 42001 certification for Australian businesses](iso-42001-certification-for-australian-businesses.md). ## FAQ

### Is AI regulated in Australia?

Not yet by a dedicated statute. Australia has no AI-specific Act in force and governs AI through existing law, chiefly the Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles, alongside consumer, anti-discrimination and sector rules. The federal Voluntary AI Safety Standard adds ten voluntary guardrails, and the government has consulted on making guardrails mandatory for high-risk AI. A governance framework is how you meet those distributed and emerging obligations deliberately.

### What is ISO/IEC 42001?

ISO/IEC 42001 is the first international standard for an AI management system, published in December 2023. It sets out how an organisation should govern the AI it builds or uses: policy, roles, risk and impact assessments, controls such as logging and human oversight, and a review cycle. A certificate against it is issued by an accredited independent body, so it signals audited governance rather than a self-description. In Australia the accreditation route runs through JAS-ANZ.

### What is the Voluntary AI Safety Standard?

It is a federal standard published in 2024 that sets out ten guardrails for safe and responsible AI, covering accountability, risk management, data governance, testing, human oversight, transparency and record-keeping. It is voluntary, but it is the clearest local statement of expected good practice, it maps onto ISO/IEC 42001, and it previews the shape of the mandatory guardrails the government has proposed for high-risk AI.

### Where does our data need to be stored to meet these obligations?

No instrument mandates a storage location. Australian Privacy Principle 8 requires that when personal information is disclosed to an overseas recipient, which happens the moment you use an offshore AI provider, you take reasonable steps to ensure comparable protection. "AI data residency" is one of the more frequent governance queries we see locally, and a governance framework forces you to document and manage those cross-border flows rather than let them happen by default.

### Do I need the NIST AI RMF and ISO 42001, or just one?

Most businesses use both, in sequence. The NIST AI RMF is the free framework you run to organise the work and operationalise the Voluntary AI Safety Standard guardrails; ISO 42001 is the certificate you add when someone wants independent proof. Because the framework produces the evidence the certificate is audited on, running the framework first makes certification cheaper and faster.

## Where to start

The honest first move is a gap analysis of your actual AI systems against the three layers, because it turns an abstract framework into one costed work list and shows which layer you are on. Businesses that already treat their AI as governed operations, with named owners, logging and a human oversight loop, tend to find the framework formalises what they do and any later certificate is a short step rather than a rebuild. Australia's direction of travel is clear enough to plan against: voluntary guardrails today, a credible prospect of mandatory ones for high-risk uses, and buyers who increasingly ask for proof before the law does.
